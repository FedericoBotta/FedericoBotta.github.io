    How transport coverage shapes access to opportunities: a national-scale isochrone analysis of bus network accessibility in England

    Sam Townley & Federico Botta

    “Everywhere in England is 40 minutes away — by car. By bus, the picture is very different.”

    Scroll to explore

    The Accessibility Gap

    Across most of England, bus reach is a small fraction of car reach

    Starting from the same point, a car can reach roughly 20–30 times more of England than a bus within 40 minutes. For most areas, the bus network covers only a tiny fraction of what a car can access — measured both by area and by number of actual destinations.

    RA — Area ratio

    61.4%

    of LSOAs reach less than 1% of the area accessible by car within a 40-minute bus journey

    RD — Destination ratio

    71%

    Both metrics tell the same story. Whether you measure raw travel area or count specific destinations (employment centres, education establishments, GP surgeries, and hospitals), bus coverage is a small fraction of what a car provides. This gap is not uniform: it is overwhelmingly concentrated in rural areas and varies sharply by region.

    The Method

    Two complementary measures of bus accessibility

    The paper introduces two new metrics that compare what you can reach in 40 minutes by bus versus by car, computed for every LSOA in England using real BODS GTFS timetables, r5py routing, and OpenStreetMap data.

    RA

    Area ratio

    bus isochrone area ÷ car isochrone area

    Measures how much geographic area you can reach by bus relative to a car. A value of 0.01 means bus covers just 1% of the car’s reach — strongly influenced by network connectivity and route directness.

    RD

    Destination ratio

    bus destinations ÷ car destinations

    Counts specific destinations within each isochrone across eight Journey Time Statistics types: employment centres (small, medium, large), education establishments (primary, secondary, further), GP surgeries, and hospitals. A value of 0.05 means you can reach 1 in 20 destinations a car user can access in the same time.

    Scale of the Study

    England-wide — every small area

    Isochrone routing was performed for all 33,755 Lower Super Output Areas in England using r5py and real BODS GTFS timetables alongside OpenStreetMap road data.

    Urban vs Rural

    The bus–car gap is overwhelmingly a rural problem

    Urban and suburban LSOAs enjoy substantially higher bus accessibility than rural ones. The share of LSOAs where bus coverage falls well below car coverage rises steeply from urban cores to remote rural areas.

    The urban–rural gradient is steep and consistent across both RA and RD. In rural villages, the overwhelming majority of LSOAs have bus coverage far below 5% of car coverage.     Regional Picture

    London leads; the South West and rural East lag furthest behind

    London’s dense bus network produces the highest median RD values in the country. Westminster, Tower Hamlets, and Waltham Forest top national rankings. Plymouth stands out as a notable exception in the generally poor-performing South West.

    Regression Findings

    What drives bus accessibility?

    A SARAR spatial regression model — accounting for spatial autocorrelation in both outcomes and errors — identifies the factors most strongly associated with an LSOA’s RD score. Spatial clustering is high: Moran’s I = 0.66 for OLS residuals, confirming that accessibility concentrates geographically.

    ↑ Positive

    Car-free household share

    The strongest predictor. Areas where more residents do not own a car tend to have better bus services — likely because demand sustains higher frequencies. Points to a self-reinforcing cycle of access and car ownership.

    ↑ Positive

    Service frequency

    Higher frequency expands effective reach. More departures let riders chain journeys and access further destinations within the 40-minute window.

    ↑ Positive

    Urban classification

    Urban LSOAs consistently outperform rural ones, independent of other factors. Network density, route coverage, and shorter inter-stop distances compound in city settings.

    — Weak / Distinct

    Deprivation index (IMD)

    RD correlates only weakly with the Index of Multiple Deprivation (r = 0.14). Bus accessibility is a distinct dimension of disadvantage — not simply a proxy for wider socioeconomic deprivation.

    Policy implication: Bus accessibility disadvantage is not automatically captured by existing deprivation measures. Areas that rank well on the IMD may still face severe bus–car gaps. Dedicated metrics like RA and RD are needed to identify where interventions would have the greatest impact.
